1. Plain-Language Summary (Sharing the Pearl)
Rebuilding a hand-carved solid-wood Polynesian instrument without factory blueprints fails whenever standard soprano or concert ukulele dimensions are assumed blindly. Using the intact green-and-natural Tahitian 8-string ukulele on the right side of bench photograph 1027.jpg (packaged inside rapa_nui_uku_luthier_bundle_v1.0.0.zip) as an internal multi-point metrological ruler—anchored by its 35.0 mm 4-on-a-plate tuner pitch and the standard 415 mm Asonu Tahitian scale length—unlocks every critical rebuild constraint of the carved Rapa Nui Moai/Tiki body on the left:


   1. Exact (2 + √2) Six-Fret Octave-Bisection Scale Lock: The carved Rapa Nui neck carries 6 original 12-TET fret kerfs across a ~47.5 mm nut and 10 peg holes (5L + 5R). Because Fret 6 bisects the octave in geometric frequency space (1 - 2^(-6/12) = 1 - 1/√2), measuring the physical distance D₀→₆ from the nut edge to Fret 6 locks the exact scale length L = (2 + √2) × D₀→₆ ≈ 3.414214 × D₀→₆ (~540.0 mm / 21.26" baritone scale at D₀→₆ ≈ 158.16 mm), placing the compensated floating bridge saddle at L + 1.8 mm (541.8 mm) above the lower-bout circular soundboard cavity (Ø 140.0 mm outer rabbet, Ø 120.0 mm clear bore).
   2. Polymer Density Scaling Prevents Re-Entrant Diaphragm Collapse: Traditional Tahitian solid-body ukuleles run uniform 0.45 mm / 20–30 lb nylon fishing line tuned to re-entrant A4–D5–F#4–B4 on a short 415 mm scale. Stretching unstepped 0.45 mm line onto a 540 mm Rapa Nui scale multiplies tension by (540 / 415)² = 1.693×, pulling 32.80 lb (145.9 N) on the D5 course alone and crushing a thin wooden diaphragm. Stepping nylon gauges (0.30–0.55 mm) or pairing PVDF fluorocarbon (ρ = 1,780 kg/m³, 1.5614× denser than Nylon 6/6 at ρ = 1,140 kg/m³) stabilizes neck and diaphragm loads across 8-string Baritone-Tahitian (E4–A4–D4–F#4, matching AUD-TRK-09), High Tahitian D6, 5-String All-Fourths (A3–D4–G4–C5–F5), and Rapa Nui–Andean 10-String (G4–C5–E5/E4–A4–E5) voicings.
   3. Reversible Banjo-Ukulele Diaphragm Retention: Rather than gluing a permanent top plate over the carved Moai/Tiki rim, seating a 1.6–2.0 mm quarter-sawn western red cedar or Sitka spruce disc (or 1.5 mm aircraft birch ply) with four cardinal brass escutcheon pins mirrors traditional Polynesian 8-string wooden banjo-ukulele flesh-hoop mechanics, allowing non-destructive diaphragm swaps while maintaining 2.0 mm Fret-6 action and an sub-4 g floating bridge.
